Simanta Nandi’s Digital Humanities Portfolio

Portfolio introduction. My work this semester keeps coming back to one question: what happens to Indian texts and languages when a machine is asked to read them? The four assignments below deal with English writing from colonial and present-day India, the small grammatical words of Bengali, the house rules of two academic publishers and a short story by Ruskin Bond. Three of them put a language model to work, and in each case I checked what it gave me against the source myself instead of taking it on trust. Most of my findings came out of that checking, because the gap between what a model says and what a text actually contains turned out to be evidence in its own right. Each exhibition below links to its project website and its GitHub repository, so you can follow the work from the first question to the data.

Assignment 01

Extraction of Indian Flora and Fauna Elements Using RAG

Abstract. This project asks a plain question. When a small language model reads Indian literature through a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline, can it tell us which plants and animals the texts name?

I built a repository of eight Indian literary texts in English: six public-domain files from Project Gutenberg, Naidu's The Bird of Time from Wikisource, and Hansda Sowvendra Shekhar's The Adivasi Will Not Dance, which is still in copyright. Each one went into AnythingLLM and was queried with Google's Gemma 3 4B, running on my own machine through LM Studio. A primary prompt asked for every tree, plant, flower, herb and crop, and every mammal, bird, reptile, fish and insect, each backed by an exact quotation. That prompt did not work well, so I wrote a second prompt with a fixed structure and filled it in for each book with its author, its title, a note on what it contains and examples from it. The answers audited on the project website come from this customised prompt, used for all eight books.

The answers looked careful, but most were not right. Of the 106 rows the model returned, only 19 were fully correct. Many of its quotations do not exist in the books. Some of its "animals" were people, a passenger jeep and chewing tobacco. To find out what the texts really contain, I read every candidate name against the full text of all eight books, using WordNet only to suggest candidates. The result is 509 text-level entries (469 verified and 40 ambiguous). Each is tied to a quotation that a script copied from the source and then re-checked word for word.

The distance between the two lists is the main finding. It opens up in five places. Retrieval showed the model four chunks of each book, which is between 1 and 7 per cent of it. The model and the lexical tools around it were shaped mostly by Western data. The colonial English of the translations had already swapped some Indian birds and trees for European ones. The customised prompt planted some of its own examples in the answers. And the ten-category scheme fits Indian ritual plants badly. The project website lists every name text by text and sets out where each kind of bias comes in.

Assignment 02

Bengali Stopwords: The Words We Read Past

Abstract. Every Bengali sentence leans on a handful of small words. Pronouns, postpositions, conjunctions, auxiliary verbs and negators carry the grammar of a sentence while saying little about what it is about. In natural language processing (NLP) these are called stop words, and they are usually removed before a text is counted, indexed or modelled.

English has had tested stop lists since the early years of information retrieval (Luhn 1958; Fox 1990). Bengali, with roughly 284 million speakers (Eberhard, Simons and Fennig 2025), has far fewer, and most of those in circulation are bare lists in Bengali script that say nothing about how the words were chosen.

This project compiles 317 Bengali stop words in romanised form and annotates each one with its Bengali-script spelling, an English gloss, a grammatical category and a register label. The words fall into 17 categories, grouped into four families: pronominals, relational words, adverbials, and verbal forms with negation. The list is released as a Python module in the format set for the course, as plain-text, CSV and JSON files, and as a website.

Three findings stand out. Pronouns and their case forms make up 42.6 per cent of the list, because Bengali marks person, number, case and three grades of politeness on the pronoun itself. The romanised list does not map cleanly onto Bengali script: 317 entries resolve to 314 distinct Bengali words, and one entry could not be matched with confidence. And a test on a short passage shows the list removing a little over half of all tokens (27 of 49), while words carrying suffixes pass through untouched. I argue that an Indic stop list is more useful when it records categories and register, so that a researcher can decide which kinds of small word to remove for a given question.

Assignment 03

PressReady: An LLM Skill That Formats Research Manuscripts

Abstract. Academic publishers do not agree on what a finished manuscript should look like. Bloomsbury's academic division prescribes nearly everything, from the shape of a quotation mark to the order of the preliminary pages. Routledge, part of Taylor & Francis, has no house style at all; it asks for consistency, an abstract for every chapter and a carefully named set of files. Students and early-career researchers who write for both usually learn these rules the slow way, through returned files and long lists of copy-editing queries.

PressReady is a modular skill that teaches a large language model to apply both sets of guidelines. I read the three official documents in full, 99 pages in all, and turned them into 632 separate rule statements. These are arranged in eleven Markdown files: a short routing file that tells the model how to work, seven rule modules, a point-by-point comparison of the two publishers, a set of checklists and a file of worked examples. The skill can be uploaded to Claude as it is, or added as knowledge files to other assistants.

A demonstration on a sample chapter shows one draft formatted for each publisher. The analysis finds that the two houses agree on only five of thirty comparable points, and that their differences follow a clear pattern: Bloomsbury governs the sentence, while Routledge governs the file.

Visible assignment work

Every task runs through the same five steps, which are written into SKILL.md:

  1. Brief: find out the publisher, the type of book, UK or US English, the referencing system and any instructions from the editor.
  2. Load: open only the modules the task needs.
  3. Audit: list every problem in a table, sorted into blocking issues, style issues and queries for the author.
  4. Apply: return the corrected text in full, changing its form but not its content, with [CHECK] placeholders wherever something is missing.
  5. Finish: tick the publisher's checklist and list the files and forms the author still has to supply.

Counting the rules made the split between the two houses easy to see. Bloomsbury has 405 rule statements, and 257 of them deal with language and referencing. Routledge has 227, and 185 of those deal with figures, front matter, permissions and file submission.

Assignment 04

Synthetic Reading: The Eyes Have It

Abstract. This project applies synthetic reading, a method of literary analysis developed by Ghosh, Shanmugapriya T and Mandal (2026) in which interpretation comes before computation, to Ruskin Bond's short story "The Eyes Have It". In the story, a young man who is blind shares a train compartment with a girl and takes care not to reveal his blindness. Only after she has left does he learn, from another passenger, that she was blind as well.

The initial impression was formed after reading only 21 of the story's sentences. Read in isolated blocks, its language of seeing looked like ordinary description, ordinary metaphor and ordinary small talk. The project therefore asks what the metaphor of sight and blindness does across the whole story, and how markers of evasion, assumption and turn-taking reveal the information asymmetry and mutual deception between the narrator and the girl. Four dimensions, with twelve sub-types, were turned into a prompt. An open-weight language model, qwen2.5:7b, extracted phrases from the story in three chunks; 49 phrases survived word-for-word verification and were counted, 42 times in all, across twelve scenes, four stages and four voices.

Four claims were stated before the full reading and tested against the counts by rules written in advance: one was partly supported (C2), two were not supported (C3, C4) and one was inconclusive (C1).

Read through phenomenology, disability studies, pragmatics and conversation analysis, the story suggests that sight and blindness work as a metaphor for knowing other people. The travellers' politeness, guesses and silences let each of them avoid knowing the other, and the final question, "Didn't you notice?", is asked of every reader who assumed that the girl could see.

Visible assignment work

I wrote the four claims down before reading the whole story, together with the rule that would decide each one. The counts then gave these verdicts:

  • C1, imagined sight gathers in the middle of the story: inconclusive. There was only one imagined-sight hit, which is too few to judge.
  • C2, pretence runs through the conversation and revelation comes at the end: partly supported. All three pretence hits fall before the girl leaves, but the model found no revelation phrases.
  • C3, the girl evades and assumes as much as the narrator: not supported. Her 135 words contain no such markers, while the narrator's words contain eight.
  • C4, turn-taking thins out after the girl leaves: not supported. The rate is 2.08 hits per 100 words in Stages I to III and 2.14 in Stage IV.

C3 failed for an interesting reason. The girl's concealment is shown by the ending, not by her vocabulary, so a count of her words cannot find it. That is where the close reading on the project website takes over.

02

Portfolio reflection

Looking back, the four projects share a habit more than a topic. Each one starts with a reading that I did myself, and only then hands part of the work to a model or a script. In the Ruskin Bond project this order was built into the method: I read 21 sentences, wrote down my impression and stated four claims before the model saw the whole story. In the flora and fauna project it happened the other way round. I trusted the pipeline first, and the audit afterwards showed me how much that trust had cost.

The biggest lesson was about evidence. A model's answer can look finished, with tidy tables, section numbers and quotation marks, and still be wrong in most of its rows. Only 19 of 106 rows held up. I now treat whatever a model returns as a list of leads to be checked against the text, and I have come to think the checking is the real research. The Bengali list taught the same thing in a quieter way. Removing small words looks like a neutral clean-up, but removing a negator can reverse a sentence, and removing pronouns of address throws away evidence about who is speaking to whom.

I also learnt that categories are never innocent. My ten categories for plants and animals came from European natural history, and they had no proper place for kusha grass or the conch. The stop list became far more useful once every word carried a category and a register label, because a researcher can then choose what to remove. Even PressReady, which looks like a purely technical job, depended on how I sorted 632 rules into modules. In the Bond project, two of my four claims were not supported and one could not be judged at all. I find that more useful than a neat confirmation, since it showed me exactly where counting words stops being enough.

Building a website for each project changed the way I wrote. A web page has readers who may never open the repository, so every figure needed a plain caption, every number needed a source and every claim needed a route back to the data. Two of my texts are still in copyright, and keeping them out of the repositories while still letting readers check my quotations was a small but real problem of public presentation. It is one I want to handle better next time.

Optional: methods note

All four projects follow the same basic workflow: read first, fix the categories, let a tool do the repetitive part, and then check everything against the source. For the flora and fauna project I used AnythingLLM with Google's Gemma 3 4B, run locally in LM Studio, and wrote Python scripts to copy and re-check every quotation. WordNet only suggested candidate names; it never decided what counted. The synthetic reading ran qwen2.5:7b through Ollama in a Google Colab notebook, at temperature 0 with a fixed seed, and kept only the phrases that appear word for word in the story.

The Bengali list was compiled by hand, and a single annotated table generates the Python module, the text, CSV and JSON files and the charts. PressReady is a set of plain Markdown files written from a close reading of 99 pages of publisher guidelines; it is not affiliated with or endorsed by Bloomsbury or Taylor & Francis. In every project the categories, scene divisions and verdicts are my own decisions, and I checked the data alone, without a second annotator.

Two texts, Hansda Sowvendra Shekhar's The Adivasi Will Not Dance and Ruskin Bond's "The Eyes Have It", are still in copyright. Neither is stored in a repository, and both are quoted only briefly. In the Bond project I read as a sighted reader and leaned on disability scholarship rather than personal experience, and I have said so on the project website.
